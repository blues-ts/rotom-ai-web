Is it worth grading your Pokémon card?

A simple way to decide before you pay: compare the raw and graded prices, judge the card honestly, and add up what grading really costs.

River TCG · Updated November 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer

Grading is worth it when the graded price you can realistically expect is higher than the raw price plus the full cost of grading. The catch is "realistically": plenty of cards don't come back a 10, so work out what the card is worth as a 9 too. If it only makes money as a 10, it's a gamble.

Step 1: Look up three prices

For the exact card you own (same set, same number, same language), find:

  • Raw price: what an ungraded copy in Near Mint condition sells for.
  • PSA 10 (or CGC 10) price: the best case.
  • PSA 9 price: the likely case for many cards.

Use recent sold prices, not listings. Asking prices on eBay can be far above what cards actually sell for. River shows raw prices and prices at each grade for every major grading company on the same screen, which makes this step quick.

Many modern cards sell for almost the same as a PSA 9 as they do raw. If that's true for your card, the whole decision rests on getting a 10.

Step 2: Judge the card honestly

Before you count on a 10, check the four things graders look at:

  • Centering. PSA 10 needs about 55/45 or better on the front. This is the easiest to measure and the one that can't be fixed. See how to check Pokémon card centering.
  • Corners. Look for any white showing at the tips, even a small speck.
  • Edges. Whitening along the back edges is common, especially on cards that were played or shuffled.
  • Surface. Tilt the card under a bright light to look for scratches, dents, print lines, and fingerprints.

A card pulled straight from a pack and sleeved has a much better chance than one that sat loose in a box. Even so, plenty of pack-fresh cards have factory problems like off-center cuts or print lines.

How often do cards get a 10?

It varies a lot by card. Across all trading card games, about half the cards PSA graded in the first half of 2025 came back as 10s, according to GemRate data reported by CLLCT. Some individual cards gem far more often than that, and some much less. That number is only an average, and the cards people choose to send in are usually their best copies, so don't use it as the odds for your own card. Your card's condition matters far more.

PSA's population report shows how many copies of a specific card have received each grade. If very few copies of your card have reached a 10, take that as a warning.

Step 3: Add up the real cost

The grading fee is only part of it. Your cost per card includes:

  • The grading fee for your service tier.
  • Shipping to the grader, and return shipping and insurance on the way back.
  • Membership fees, if the tier needs one.
  • Selling fees later, if you plan to sell. Marketplaces usually take around 10 to 15% of the sale price.

Step 4: Do the math

Here's a worked example with made-up but realistic numbers. Say your card is worth:

  • $40 raw
  • $55 as a PSA 9
  • $150 as a PSA 10

You send it with PSA Value at $39.99 and figure about $5 per card for shipping and insurance, so grading costs about $45. To come out ahead, the graded card has to be worth more than $85: the $40 you could have sold it for raw, plus the $45 you spent.

If the card is clean and well centered

Say you think it has a 50% chance at a 10, 40% at a 9, and 10% at an 8 (worth $35). The expected value is:

0.5 × $150 + 0.4 × $55 + 0.1 × $35 = $100.50

That's about $15 more than the $85 you need, so grading makes sense, although you'll lose money on any copy that comes back a 9.

If the same card is 60/40

A 60/40 front rules out a PSA 10, so the best realistic case is a 9:

0.8 × $55 + 0.2 × $35 = $51

That's well under $85. You'd spend $45 to turn a $40 card into one worth about $51. Keep it raw.

When grading usually makes sense

  • The PSA 10 price is several times the raw price, and the card has a real chance at a 10.
  • Even a PSA 9 is worth clearly more than raw plus grading costs.
  • The card is valuable enough that buyers want it authenticated, as with older or high-end cards.
  • You want to keep the card long-term and value the protection of a slab more than the cost.

When it usually doesn't

  • The card is worth less than the grading fee itself.
  • The centering is clearly outside 10 range and a 9 sells for about the same as raw.
  • There's visible whitening, a scratch, or a dent. Graders will see it.
  • You need the money soon. Cheaper tiers take months, and prices can drop while you wait.

Questions

How much does it cost to grade a Pokémon card?

As of November 2026, PSA's cheapest tier (Value) is $39.99 per card for cards worth up to $500, and CGC's Bulk tier is $15 per card with a 25-card minimum. Shipping both ways, insurance, and any membership fees come on top, so the real cost per card is higher than the listed price.

Should I grade with PSA or CGC?

Check the graded prices for your exact card. PSA slabs usually sell for more, especially at PSA 10, which can make PSA worth its higher fee on valuable cards. CGC is cheaper per card, which can make more sense when the gap between raw and graded prices is small.

What declared value should I put on my submission?

Use what you expect the card to be worth after grading, not what you paid for it. Each service tier has a maximum declared value, and if the graded card is worth more than your tier allows, the grading company can move it to a more expensive tier and charge you the difference.

How long does grading take?

Usually months at the cheaper tiers. As of November 2026, PSA estimates 90 to 100 business days for its Value tier. Faster tiers cost much more. Expect prices to move while you wait, in either direction.
