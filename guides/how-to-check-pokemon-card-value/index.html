How much is my Pokémon card worth?

Three steps to a real number: find the exact printing, judge its condition, and look at what copies have actually sold for.

River TCG · Updated November 24, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer

A Pokémon card is worth what the same printing, in the same condition, has recently sold for. Not what people are asking for it, and not what a different version of the same Pokémon goes for. Get those three things right (exact card, condition, sold prices) and you have a fair value.

Step 1: Identify the exact card

Two cards with the same Pokémon and the same artwork can be worth wildly different amounts. Before you look up any price, write down:

  • The set. Look for the small set symbol, usually near the bottom of the card. The original 1999 Base Set is the exception: it has no set symbol at all.
  • The collector number. Something like 025/198. A number higher than the set total (like 201/198) means a secret rare.
  • The rarity symbol. A circle is common, a diamond is uncommon, and a star is rare. Newer sets add more symbols, such as two black stars or gold stars, for higher rarities.
  • The language. Japanese and English versions of the same card are priced separately, and the gap can go either way.

Check the variant

Many cards were printed in more than one version, and the version often matters more than anything else:

  • Holo vs. reverse holo. On a holo, the shine is in the artwork. On a reverse holo, the artwork is flat and the rest of the card shines. They're priced separately.
  • 1st Edition. Older Wizards of the Coast era cards with a small "Edition 1" stamp, usually just below the artwork on the left, sell for more than the unlimited version, sometimes many times more.
  • Shadowless. Early Base Set prints have no drop shadow along the right side of the art box. They're worth more than the later prints that have one.
  • Promo stamps. Stamps like a prerelease or event logo make a card a different item from the regular print.
  • Reprints. Base Set 2, Legendary Collection and the 2021 Celebrations Classic Collection reused older artwork, but they're different cards with their own markings and their own prices.

This is the step most people get wrong. If you look up "Charizard" and take the first price you see, you're probably pricing a different card.

Step 2: Judge its condition

Prices for ungraded ("raw") cards are usually given by condition. The common scale is:

ConditionWhat it looks like
Near Mint (NM)Looks fresh from the pack. At most a tiny flaw you have to look for.
Lightly Played (LP)Small amounts of edge or corner wear, or a light scratch.
Moderately Played (MP)Wear you notice right away: whitening, scuffs, minor creasing.
Heavily Played (HP)Lots of wear, creases or bends, but the card is intact.
DamagedTears, water damage, writing, or a heavy crease.

Each step down usually takes a real bite out of the price, and the drop tends to be steeper for valuable cards. Look at the card under a bright light and check the back edges, corners and surface. Be honest: most people rate their own cards a grade higher than a buyer would.

If the card is in a PSA, CGC or other graded slab, ignore the raw prices and look up the price for that grader and grade instead. If you're wondering whether to get a raw card graded, see is it worth grading your Pokémon card?

Step 3: Look up sold prices, not asking prices

Anyone can list a card for any price. What matters is what buyers actually paid. Good places to look:

  • eBay sold listings. Search for the card, then turn on the Sold items filter. Check that each sale is the same set, number, variant and condition as yours.
  • TCGplayer market price. Based on recent completed sales on TCGplayer, listed separately for each condition and variant.
  • Price guides that track sales, like PriceCharting, which also show graded prices and history over time.

Look at several recent sales, not just one. A single high sale can be a fluke, and prices for popular cards can move a lot in a few weeks. If a card has very few sales, any price is a rough guess.

What you'll actually get for it

Market price is what a card sells for between collectors. What lands in your pocket depends on how you sell:

  • Selling it yourself online gets closest to market price, but marketplace fees (often around 10 to 15%) and shipping come out of it.
  • Selling to a shop or a vendor at a card show is fast, but expect an offer well below market price. They need room to resell it. Store credit or trade value is usually better than cash.
  • Trading works on market value for both sides, so agree on a price source first.

Common mistakes

  • Pricing from asking prices instead of sold prices.
  • Mixing up holo and reverse holo, or 1st Edition and unlimited.
  • Pricing a reprint as if it were the original.
  • Calling a played card Near Mint.
  • Using a PSA 10 price for a raw card. A raw card is only worth a graded price after it's graded.

Questions

How do I know if my Pokémon card is valuable?

Some signs point to a card worth checking: a star rarity symbol or a special rarity like a full art or illustration rare, a 1st Edition stamp, an older Wizards of the Coast era card, or a holo of a popular Pokémon. But the only way to know is to look up recent sold prices for that exact card in its condition.

Are old Pokémon cards worth money?

Some are worth a lot, but most commons and uncommons from the late 1990s sell for very little. Age alone doesn't make a card valuable. Rarity, the Pokémon on it, the exact printing (such as 1st Edition or shadowless) and condition decide the price.

What's the most accurate way to price a Pokémon card?

Use recent sold prices for the exact card, in the same language, variant and condition. Asking prices on listings are often much higher than what cards actually sell for, and a different printing of the same Pokémon can be worth far more or far less.

Will a card shop pay me the market price?

Usually not. Shops need room to resell, so cash offers are normally well below market price. Store credit or trade value is usually higher than cash. Selling yourself gets closer to market price, but marketplace fees and shipping come out of it.
