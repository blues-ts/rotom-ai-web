How to spot fake Pokémon cards

Most fakes fail a few simple checks. Here's what to look at, from the easy ones you can do anywhere to the tests for cards worth real money.

River TCG · Updated November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

The fastest way to catch a fake is to compare it with a card you know is real, ideally from the same set. Check the text and symbols, the blue back, the size, and the surface finish. If it still looks right but the card is worth a lot, do the light test, or buy it already graded and look up the slab's cert number.

Start with a real card to compare

Every check below is easier side by side. Grab a cheap common you pulled from a pack yourself, ideally from the same set or era, and hold the two cards together. Many fakes that look fine on their own are obvious next to the real thing.

Also look up the official version of the card: same set, same collector number. If the name, attacks, HP or artwork on your card don't match the official card exactly, you have your answer.

1. Read the text

Counterfeiters often get the words wrong. Look for:

  • Spelling and grammar mistakes in attack names or descriptions.
  • "Pokemon" without the accent. Real English cards always print "Pokémon".
  • A font that looks off: too thin, too bold, wrongly spaced, or slightly blurry.
  • Numbers that don't make sense, like a Pikachu with 1000 HP or an attack that does 999 damage.
  • Wrong or blurry Energy symbols next to attacks.

2. Check the back

The back is the same on every English card, so it's one of the best tells. On fakes, the blue is often too light, too purple or too flat, the swirl looks smudged, and the Poké Ball or logo is slightly the wrong shape or size. Put it next to your real card and look at the colors in good light.

3. Feel the card and check the size

Real Pokémon cards measure 63 × 88 mm (about 2.5 × 3.5 in) and have a firm, slightly flexible feel. Fakes are often thinner and floppier, or very glossy and slippery. Stack the card on top of your real one: the edges should line up exactly.

4. Look at the holo and texture

  • Holo pattern. On a real holo, the shine follows a specific pattern in the right places. Fakes often have a generic rainbow shine across the whole card, or a holo pattern from a different era.
  • Texture. Modern ultra rares, like full arts and illustration rares, have a fine etched texture you can feel with your fingertip and see when you tilt the card. Fakes are usually smooth and flat.

5. Check the set symbol, number and rarity

Make sure the set symbol, collector number and rarity symbol all agree. A fake might print a number the set doesn't have, use a rarity symbol that doesn't fit the card, or leave off the set symbol entirely. (The original 1999 Base Set is the one real exception: it has no set symbol.) For a walkthrough of where these sit on the card, see how to check what your card is worth.

6. Try the light test

Real Pokémon cards are made from layers of paper with a dark layer in the middle that blocks most light. Many fakes are printed on plain card stock that doesn't have that layer.

In a dark room, hold your phone's flashlight right behind the card. A real card lets through only a dim glow. A fake often lights up bright. Do the same with your known real card so you know what "normal" looks like.

The light test isn't perfect. How much light gets through varies between eras and print runs, and better fakes can pass. Use it alongside the other checks, not on its own.

You may also hear about the "rip test": tearing a card to see the dark middle layer. It works, but it destroys the card, so it's only useful for checking a cheap card from a suspect bulk lot.

7. Use a magnifier

A jeweler's loupe or your phone's camera zoomed in shows how the card was printed. Real cards show a neat pattern of tiny color dots, crisp edges on text, and clean lines. Fakes often look blurry or blotchy, with fuzzy text and colors bleeding into each other.

8. Be suspicious of the deal

Where and how a card is sold tells you a lot:

  • Too cheap to be true. A chase card well below its usual sold price is a red flag.
  • Bulk lots of only ultra rares, like "50 GX and V cards for $10", are almost always fake.
  • Gold, metal or black "special" cards sold in lots are not official. The Pokémon Company has only released metal cards in a few specific premium products.
  • Stock photos instead of photos of the actual card, or a seller who won't send more pictures.

9. Verify graded slabs

Fake slabs exist too: a fake card in a copied PSA, CGC or Beckett case, or a real label swapped onto a different card. Before you buy a graded card:

  • Look up the cert number on the grader's website. It should show the same card, grade and, where the grader has them, matching photos.
  • Check the label and case against a slab you know is real: fonts, holograms, logo and how the case is sealed.
  • Make sure the card inside matches the label, including the set and variant.

If you think you bought a fake

If you bought it online, contact the seller and open a return or a buyer-protection claim with the marketplace or your payment method. Most have time limits, so do it right away. Take clear photos of the card next to a real one showing the differences. And don't sell or trade the fake on to someone else.

Questions

How can you tell if a Pokémon card is fake?

Compare it with a card you know is real, ideally from the same set. Check for spelling mistakes and the wrong font, a back that's the wrong shade of blue, the wrong size or a flimsy feel, a holo pattern or texture that doesn't match, and a set symbol, number or rarity that doesn't fit. For valuable cards, do the light test or buy them graded.

Does the light test work on every card?

No. Real cards have a dark middle layer, so they let through much less light than most fakes, but the amount varies between eras and print runs, and better fakes can pass. Compare against a real card from a similar era and use it together with other checks.

Are gold metal Pokémon cards real?

Almost all gold or metal Pokémon cards sold in lots online are unofficial. The Pokémon Company has only released metal cards in a few specific premium products, so treat any gold card sold cheaply or in bulk as fake.

Can a graded Pokémon card be fake?

Yes. Counterfeit slabs and swapped labels exist. Look up the cert number on the grader's website and check that the card, grade and photos match, and compare the label and case with a slab you know is real.
