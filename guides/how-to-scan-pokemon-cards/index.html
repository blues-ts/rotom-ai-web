How to scan Pokémon cards with your phone

A card scanner app can price a whole stack in minutes, as long as you give it a good picture and check what it found.

River TCG · Updated November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer

Put the card on a plain, dark surface, use bright light from the side rather than straight above, and hold your phone flat over the card so it fills most of the screen. Then check that the app picked the right set, number, language and variant before you trust the price.

What a card scanner does

A scanner app uses your phone's camera to recognize a card, match it to a database of printings, and show its market price. Most can also add what you scan to a collection, so you can track its total value.

Recognition is good at telling one card from another, but some versions of the same card look almost identical on camera, like a holo and a reverse holo, or a 1st Edition and an unlimited copy. So treat the scan as a fast first match, and check the details that change the price.

Set up for a clean scan

Most failed or wrong scans come down to the picture. A minute of setup saves a lot of rescanning:

  • Use a plain, dark background. A playmat, a dark desk or a sheet of black paper makes the card's edges easy to find. Avoid patterned tablecloths and other cards in the frame.
  • Light it from the side. A bright window or a lamp off to one side gives even light without bouncing straight back into the camera. A ceiling light directly above causes glare.
  • Hold the phone flat. Keep the phone parallel to the card, not at an angle, so the card looks like a rectangle and not a trapezoid.
  • Fill the frame. Move close enough that the card takes up most of the screen, then hold still for a moment so it can focus.
  • Wipe the lens. A smudged camera lens makes everything soft and hazy.

Holos, sleeves and toploaders

Shiny cards and plastic both reflect light. If you see a bright spot on the card, tilt the card or your phone slightly until it moves off the artwork, or turn the lamp. Penny sleeves usually scan fine. Thick toploaders and graded slabs reflect more, so it can help to take the card out of a toploader if you're having trouble.

Scanning one card vs. a whole stack

If you're pricing a single card, scan it and read the result. If you're going through a stack, look for an app that keeps scanning: you place a card, it's recognized, you swap in the next one, and everything goes into a list you review at the end. That's much faster than opening each card one at a time.

A few habits keep stack scanning accurate:

  • Do one card at a time, and move the last one fully out of view before the next.
  • Sort by language first if you have both English and Japanese cards, and set the app's language to match.
  • Glance at each match as you go. It's easier to fix a wrong match now than to find it later.

Scanning binder pages

Some apps can recognize a whole nine-pocket binder page in one shot. Lay the binder flat, light it evenly from the side, and make sure all nine cards are in the frame. Binder pages are glossy, so glare is the main problem: if one card won't match, tilt the binder a little and scan again, or scan that card on its own.

Check the match before you trust the price

A scan is only as good as the card it matched. Before you use the price, compare the result with the card in your hand:

  • Set and collector number. Popular Pokémon have dozens of cards with similar art across sets and promos.
  • Language. English and Japanese versions are priced separately.
  • Variant. Holo or reverse holo, 1st Edition or unlimited, a promo stamp or not. Pick the right one if the app lets you.
  • Condition or grade. Scanner prices usually start at Near Mint for raw cards. If your card is played, or in a graded slab, switch to the matching condition or grade.

When a card won't scan

  • Glare or a busy background. Fix the light and background first. That solves most problems.
  • A brand-new set. Cards released in the last few days may not be in the app's database yet.
  • Damaged or altered cards. Heavy creases, missing pieces, or custom art can stop a match.
  • Fakes and custom cards. A card that doesn't match anything could be a counterfeit. See how to spot fake Pokémon cards.

If it still won't match, most apps let you search by name and pick the card yourself.

A note on privacy

Some scanner apps upload the camera picture to a server to recognize the card. Others do it on your phone. If that matters to you, check the app's privacy policy. River recognizes cards on your iPhone, and camera frames and scan photos aren't uploaded.

Questions

Can I scan Pokémon cards for free?

Yes. Many scanner apps let you scan cards and see a basic price for free, with extras like condition and graded prices or bulk scanning in a paid tier. In River, scanning and the Near Mint market price are free.

Are card scanner prices accurate?

They're as accurate as the match and the price source. Check that the app found the right set, number, language and variant, and that the price matches your card's condition or grade. For expensive cards, also look at recent sold listings.

Can I scan Japanese Pokémon cards?

Many apps support Japanese cards, but not all. Set the app's language to match the card, since English and Japanese versions are separate cards with separate prices. River supports both English and Japanese cards.

Can I scan cards in sleeves or toploaders?

Usually. Penny sleeves rarely cause problems. Toploaders and graded slabs reflect more light, so tilt the card slightly to move glare off the artwork, or take it out of the toploader if it won't scan.
